## Climate & Environment Projects

### Case: Circular Kolkata Alliance (Kolkata Hub, India)
Challenge: Kolkata had waste mountains as high as 16 meters. The hub needed to create behavior change in a city highly vulnerable to climate issues.
Approach: Partnered with the Climate Reality Incubator Program. Conducted 10 workshops with over 30 organizations and institutions focused on changing environmentally harmful production and consumption systems.
Result: Collected more than 20,000 kg of waste for reuse, repurpose, upcycling, and recycling. Created ongoing workshop series for sustained behavior change.
Key learning: partner with established institutions (30+ orgs) to create systemic change rather than running isolated events. Volume of partnerships matters more than size of individual partnerships.

### Case: Climate Conversations (Manchester Hub, UK)
Challenge: Understanding local perceptions of climate risk in Greater Manchester.
Approach: Supported by The Climate Reality Project (Al Gore's organization). Gathered insights from local residents about perceived climate risks and gaps in climate action.
Result: Created actionable data about community climate awareness that informed local policy discussions.
Key learning: framing climate projects as "listening to the community" rather than "lecturing the community" dramatically increases participation and buy-in.

### Case: Climate Reality Certification (Global — 1,000+ Shapers)
Challenge: How to rapidly upskill community members on climate action across many countries simultaneously.
Approach: WEF partnered with The Climate Reality Project to offer certified training. Structured as cohort-based learning with clear certification upon completion.
Result: 1,000 Shapers became certified climate action leaders in a single year. Over 400 volunteer-led climate projects were subsequently implemented worldwide.
Key learning: certification and cohort structure dramatically increase completion rates. People finish things when there's a credential at the end and peers going through it together.

### Case: Reforestation Initiative (Abuja Hub, Nigeria)
Challenge: Accelerating reforestation in an urban environment with limited resources.
Approach: Received funding through Climate Reality Innovation Grant Challenge (launched 2021). Used grant to organize community tree-planting events.
Result: Part of the network-wide effort that planted over 2 million trees globally.
Key learning: small grants ($500-2000) with clear deliverables and reporting requirements produce disproportionate impact when distributed across many local teams.

## Social Impact & Inclusion Projects

### Case: Shred The Debt (Multiple US Hubs)
Challenge: Medical debt silently burdens millions of Americans, preventing financial stability.
Approach: Innovative collaboration with Undue Medical Debt, a nonprofit that purchases medical debt from collections at pennies on the dollar. Hubs raise funds locally, then use those funds to purchase and forgive medical debt at a fraction of its original cost.
Result: A replicable framework that any US hub can adopt. For every $1 raised, approximately $100 of medical debt is eliminated.
Key learning: the most powerful projects aren't always original — they're frameworks that can be replicated across hubs. This project's strength is its "copy-paste" model: any hub can run it with minimal customization.

### Case: Hlumela Voice (Johannesburg Hub, South Africa)
Challenge: Female entrepreneurs aged 18-35 face limited access to funding, mentorship, and business networks.
Approach: Targeted program empowering 30 female entrepreneurs with training and network access.
Result: Created a cohort of supported female entrepreneurs with ongoing mentorship relationships.
Key learning: small, focused cohorts (30 people) with deep engagement outperform large events with shallow engagement for skill-building projects.

### Case: Panin Jai — Own Space (Srinagar Hub, India)
Challenge: Creating safe spaces for community dialogue in conflict-affected regions.
Approach: Community-centered approach emphasizing dialogue and shared space creation.
Key learning: in sensitive political environments, hubs succeed by focusing on human connection rather than political advocacy. The project's name "Own Space" reflects the principle that communities need to feel ownership over the change process.

## Disaster Response & Crisis

### Case: Nepal Earthquake Response (Kathmandu Hub)
Challenge: Devastating earthquake in 2015 required immediate community response.
Approach: Hub mobilized quickly to raise funds and coordinate relief. Leveraged global network for fundraising reach.
Result: Raised over $500,000. Built a school and multiple health clinics.
Key learning: hubs with strong pre-existing community bonds respond faster to crises. The relationships built through routine projects became the infrastructure for emergency response. You can't build a disaster response network during a disaster — you build it before, through regular community engagement.

### Case: COVID-19 Response (158 Hubs Globally, 2020)
Challenge: Global pandemic requiring localized responses across diverse contexts.
Approach: 158 hubs independently organized public health projects. 102 focused on supporting health systems and protecting livelihoods. 56 focused on amplifying access to trusted public health information.
Result: Distributed 114,175 units of PPE, 452,300 sanitary items. Cooked 407,383 meals. Donated 143,992 kg of groceries. Spread 25,325 evidence-based information messages. Efforts were recognized by the World Health Organization.
Key learning: decentralized networks outperform centralized organizations in crisis response. Each hub knew its local context and could act immediately without waiting for headquarters approval. The network's value was coordination and resource-sharing, not top-down direction.

## Education & Reskilling

### Case: Read to Rise (Adamawa Hub, Nigeria)
Challenge: Limited literacy rates and access to quality education in post-insurgency communities. Boys at risk of extremist recruitment, girls facing gender-based violence.
Approach: Developed evidence-based accelerated education model using Mavis Pen and book-box library systems. Piloted Teaching at the Right Level (TaRL) model for 6 months using EGRA tests for baseline literacy assessments.
Result: Measurable improvements in literacy outcomes for underprivileged children in IDP camps and underserved communities.
Key learning: technology-assisted learning (Mavis Pen) combined with human mentoring is more effective than either alone. The "right level" approach — meeting students where they are instead of where they should be — is critical for fragile settings.

### Case: Development Trainings (Shamakhi Hub, Azerbaijan)
Challenge: Young people in smaller cities lack access to professional development opportunities available in capitals.
Approach: Regular training workshops bringing professional development content to underserved areas.
Key learning: hubs in smaller cities often have higher per-capita impact than those in major capitals because they're filling a bigger gap. The training isn't novel — the access is.

## Mental Health

### Case: Shapers for Mental Health (Cross-Hub Collaboration)
Challenge: Mental health is stigmatized globally. One in four people live with a mental health disorder (450 million+ people). There are only 9 mental health workers per 100,000 people worldwide.
Approach: Created a train-the-trainer system. Shapers take a Basic Psychological First Aid course (based on WHO principles), adapt it for local context, then train others in their communities. Any hub can join by having at least 3 members participate and submitting a project plan.
Result: Scaled across multiple hubs worldwide. Established a sustainable model where each trained Shaper becomes a trainer.
Key learning: train-the-trainer models are the most scalable approach for volunteer organizations. Instead of one central team trying to reach everyone, you create multipliers. Each hub adapts the core curriculum to their local language, culture, and needs — which actually improves effectiveness.

## Cross-Hub Collaboration Models

### Case: SHAPE Regional Events (6 events globally, 2024)
Challenge: Connecting hubs across regions for knowledge exchange and collaboration.
Approach: Six regional SHAPE events gathering hubs by geography. Combined formal presentations with informal networking.
Result: 1,000+ young people participated. Generated cross-hub project ideas and ongoing collaboration.
Key learning: regional events are more effective than global ones for sparking actual collaboration. Hubs in similar time zones and cultural contexts are more likely to sustain partnerships after an event.

### Case: Cross-Hub Exchanges (Taipei with Chengdu, Hong Kong, Guangzhou)
Challenge: Asia-Pacific hubs solving similar problems independently without sharing solutions.
Approach: Virtual sharing sessions where each hub presented one success story and one current challenge. Informal networking time scheduled after formal agenda.
Result: Direct knowledge transfer. Solutions that took one hub months were adopted by others in weeks.
Key learning: the most valuable part of cross-hub exchange isn't the formal presentation — it's the "we had the exact same problem" moments that happen in informal conversation. Always schedule unstructured time after structured sessions.

## Hub Operations Insights (from WEF Research)

### What separates thriving hubs from struggling ones

Research across the Global Shapers network (conducted by Accenture and Cuantix, surveying 2,600+ Shapers) identified these success factors:

1. Maintaining momentum is more important than waiting for perfect conditions. Hubs that paused during difficulties lost members and struggled to recover.

2. Purpose and meaningfulness drive retention more than perks or prestige. Members who feel their work matters stay longer than members who joined for the WEF brand name.

3. Successful hub leaders share one trait: confidence paired with genuine humility and willingness to ask for help. "Superhuman resilience" is not required.

4. Hubs must implement at least one project per year. The act of shipping keeps the community alive even more than the project's direct impact.

5. Warm leadership handoffs (1-2 month overlap between outgoing and incoming curators) prevent knowledge loss. Cold transitions where the new curator inherits a folder of documents instead of relationships are the #1 cause of hub stagnation.

### Membership expectations that work
The most effective hubs set clear expectations upfront. Common standards across successful hubs include 8 hours per month minimum contribution, 75% meeting attendance, a 3-month trial period for new members, and participation in at least one global initiative per year.

### Hub governance structure
Effective hubs distribute leadership across roles: Curator (direction and external narrative), Vice Curator (internal operations), Impact Officer (project health), and additional roles as needed. The curatorship is not about being the boss — it's about enabling everyone else to lead.
